How Accurate Are Height Predictor Calculators for Boys?

By The Growth Path Editorial Team · Reviewed September 2026

Height predictor calculators deliver ranges, not guarantees. Simple formulas typically get within 2-3 inches of adult height on average. Complex methods with bone age are more accurate but still not exact.

The short answer

Adult-height predictions are estimates with real uncertainty. Simple mid-parental height formulas typically get within 2-3 inches of actual adult height. Methods that use bone age and current growth data (like Khamis-Roche or the Bayley-Pinneau method) are more accurate, often within 1-2 inches. No calculator is exact.

The methods calculators actually use

  • Mid-parental height. The simplest and most common. (mom's height + dad's height + 13) / 2 for boys, plus or minus about 8.5 cm. Requires only parents' heights.
  • Khamis-Roche method. Uses current height, weight, and parents' heights. No x-ray needed. Typically within 2 inches for boys.
  • Bayley-Pinneau method. Requires a bone age x-ray. Uses current height and skeletal maturity to project. Historically the most accurate non-clinical method.
  • Roche-Wainer-Thissen (RWT). Combines height, weight, parents' heights, and bone age. Highest accuracy but requires x-ray.

Typical error ranges

  • Mid-parental height: plus or minus 8.5 cm (about 3.3 inches).
  • Khamis-Roche: plus or minus 2 inches for most boys, wider for late-maturing or off-percentile boys.
  • Bayley-Pinneau with bone age: plus or minus 1-2 inches for most boys.

None of these is a promise. Any single estimate has a real chance of being off by more than the "typical" range.

Why predictions get less accurate for some boys

  • Late maturers. Boys with constitutional delay grow later than models expect. Predictions often underestimate their adult height.
  • Off-percentile trajectories. Boys whose percentile is currently dropping or climbing violate the model's assumption of stable tracking.
  • Very young ages. Predictions before age 8 are highly variable. The younger the child, the wider the range.
  • Genetic or medical conditions. Turner, Marfan, growth hormone deficiency, and others produce trajectories the models were not built for.

What actually matters more than any prediction

  • His growth velocity. Is he growing at an age-appropriate rate? That is the strongest signal of health.
  • His trend on the growth chart. Is he tracking his percentile line?
  • Puberty stage. Where he sits on the puberty timeline matters more than his birthday.

How to use a predictor calculator wisely

  • Treat the number as a range, not a target.
  • Do not celebrate or grieve. He is where he is.
  • Recheck once a year, not once a month.
  • If the prediction is far from what his current percentile suggests, look for the reason (delayed puberty, off-percentile trajectory) before assuming the number is right or wrong.
