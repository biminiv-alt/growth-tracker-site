Can Playing Sports Affect a Boy's Growth?

By The Growth Path Editorial Team · Reviewed September 2026

Normal sports participation does not stunt growth. Extreme training combined with under-eating can slow growth in some young athletes. Here is where the line actually is.

The short answer

For nearly every boy playing recreational or competitive sports, no. Regular physical activity is neutral or slightly positive for growth. It supports bone density, sleep, appetite, and overall health.

The narrow exception is a specific pattern seen mainly in very high-volume elite training combined with chronic under-eating (Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport, or RED-S). This is not typical youth or high school sports.

Where the "sports stunt growth" idea comes from

Studies of elite young gymnasts and elite young wrestlers, who trained many hours per week and often maintained low body weight, have shown slower growth and delayed puberty in some athletes. This is the source of the stereotype. Two important caveats:

  • The effect is tied to energy availability (calories in vs. calories burned), not the sport itself.
  • Elite gymnasts also tend to have shorter parents, since shorter kids often self-select into and succeed in gymnastics. Some of the height difference is genetics, not the sport.

Sports that concern pediatricians most

  • Elite-level gymnastics with 20+ hours/week training.
  • Wrestlers who cut weight to compete in a lower class.
  • Long-distance running with restricted calorie intake.
  • Any sport where the child is chronically underweight for his frame.

What these have in common: energy deficit, not the specific activity.

What actually helps growth in athletes

  • Eating enough. Active teen boys often need 2,800-4,000 calories a day.
  • Adequate protein at each meal.
  • Enough sleep (growth hormone releases during deep sleep).
  • Rest days each week.
  • Off-season with reduced training volume.

When it is worth a conversation

  • Percentile dropping after starting a high-volume training program.
  • Weight stalling or dropping in a boy who should be gaining.
  • Missed periods for female athletes, or delayed puberty in males.
  • Chronic injuries or stress fractures.

Recreational sports, school teams, and normal competitive youth sports do not stunt growth. Weight-lifting with reasonable programming does not stunt growth either. Under-eating during heavy training does.
