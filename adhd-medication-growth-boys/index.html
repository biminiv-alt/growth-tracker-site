Can ADHD Medication Affect a Boy’s Growth?

Stimulant ADHD medications can slightly slow growth in some children, and the effect is well-studied. Here is the honest magnitude, why it happens, and how pediatricians manage it.

The short answer

Yes, stimulant ADHD medications (methylphenidate, amphetamine-based) can modestly slow growth in some children. Systematic reviews and long-term studies show:

  • Slight reduction in growth velocity during the first 1-3 years of treatment.
  • Average final adult height reduction: about 1 inch, sometimes less.
  • Many children partially or fully catch up over time.
  • Non-stimulant ADHD medications (atomoxetine, guanfacine) have less effect on growth.

The effect is real but modest and needs to be weighed against the significant benefits of treating ADHD when medication is indicated. This is a conversation to have with the pediatrician or prescribing clinician, not a reason to stop medication unilaterally.

Why stimulants can affect growth

The main mechanism appears to be appetite suppression. Stimulants reduce hunger, especially at lunchtime, which can lead to lower daily calorie intake. Undernutrition during growing years slows growth. Some evidence also suggests direct effects on growth hormone signaling, but appetite suppression is the biggest known driver.

The magnitude in real numbers

  • First year of treatment: growth velocity often 0.5-1 cm slower than expected.
  • By year 3: the gap narrows for most children.
  • Final adult height: on average 1-2 cm below what genetics alone would predict. Individual variation is wide.
  • Children on higher doses or continuous year-round use show slightly more effect than those on lower doses or medication holidays.

How pediatricians manage it

  • Track growth quarterly during the first year of treatment. Weight and height at each visit.
  • Monitor calorie intake. High-calorie breakfast before medication kicks in. High-calorie snacks in the evening when appetite returns.
  • Consider medication holidays. Weekends or summers off for some children, if clinically appropriate. This is a case-by-case decision.
  • Switch to non-stimulants if growth is significantly affected and the child still needs treatment.
  • Dose optimization. Lowest effective dose reduces the effect.

Signs that ADHD medication is affecting growth

  • Percentile crossing lines downward after starting medication.
  • Growth velocity below age-appropriate range after 6-12 months on medication.
  • Weight loss or failure to gain weight during the first year.
  • Ongoing low appetite despite active management.

When to talk to the prescribing clinician

  • You notice any of the signs above.
  • His percentile has been dropping since starting medication.
  • He is skipping meals regularly.
  • You want to weigh stimulant vs. non-stimulant options.

What NOT to do

  • Do not stop the medication without talking to the prescribing clinician. Untreated ADHD has real costs (academic, social, safety).
  • Do not assume any short stature in a boy on ADHD medication is caused by the medication. Family history and puberty timing still matter more than the medication does.
  • Do not chase growth with over-the-counter supplements. None have been shown to counteract medication effects on growth.
