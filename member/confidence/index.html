Confidence and communication

Signed in as

Talking to your son about size is one of the parts of this that has no formula. What follows is honest guidance, organized by age, plus a few scripts for the moments that catch you off guard.

One principle for every age. Kids read our anxiety long before they parse our words. If size is a place of stress at home, they will inherit that. If it is a place where you are steady and curious with them, they get to be curious too.

Ages 5 to 8

What they usually notice

Kids this age notice difference. They will name it plainly: "I am the shortest in my class." They are not looking for a fix. They are checking whether it is a problem.

What tends to help

  • Match their tone. If they say it lightly, you can say it lightly back. Do not turn a passing comment into a heavy conversation.
  • Normalize different bodies. "Everyone grows at their own pace. Your body is doing what it is supposed to do." True and calm.
  • Point at other kinds of difference (hair color, freckles, being a fast runner) so height is not the only variable in the room.

Ages 9 to 12

What changes

Comparison gets sharper. Puberty starts showing up in the classroom, unevenly. A boy who is still on the pre-pubertal curve while friends are shooting up will notice.

What tends to help

  • Name the timing, not the outcome. "Puberty starts at different times for everyone. Yours will come. Your body is on its own clock."
  • Share your own timing if you can. "I was one of the smaller kids until I was 15. Then it changed."
  • Give them something concrete they can influence. Sleep, protein at meals, being active. These matter and they give kids agency without making size a moral issue.
  • Watch for withdrawal. If he stops wanting to go to school, changes friends, or drops activities he used to like, that is worth talking about with the pediatrician.

Age 13 and up

What changes

The stakes feel higher. Dating, sports positions, and social standing all get filtered through size for some boys. He may not want to talk to you. He is not being difficult, he is being 13.

What tends to help

  • Be a reliable audience without pushing. Short car rides and side-by-side activities produce more conversation than sit-down talks.
  • If he brings it up, resist the urge to fix. Reflect first ("that sounds frustrating") before offering anything.
  • Be honest about the range of outcomes. Some late bloomers catch up fully. Some end up in the shorter range of average. Both are okay.
  • If he asks whether there is anything medical to do, tell him you will bring it up together with the pediatrician. That keeps you on the same team.

Talking points for teachers and coaches

Most teachers and coaches want to do right by your son but need one specific ask. A short email is usually enough.

Sample email to a teacher:

Hi [Name], quick note about [son's name]. He is on the smaller side for his age right now, and we are tracking his growth with his pediatrician. He is doing well and we are not worried. If you notice anything socially, please let us know. Otherwise please treat him like every other kid in the class. Thank you.

Sample note to a coach:

Hi Coach, [son's name] loves playing and wants to keep improving. Please give him the same feedback and challenge as anyone else. If a positional decision is coming down to size, we would rather he hear the real reason than a softened version.

Scripts for hard moments

He comes home upset that a teammate called him small

First: "That is a rough thing to hear. Are you okay?"

Then: "It is true that you are shorter than [teammate] right now. It is also true that you are a good [player/friend/kid], and none of that is because of your height."

Later, not in the same conversation: "If you ever want to talk about how it feels to be smaller than a lot of your friends, I am here. And if you do not, that is fine too."

He asks, "Why am I smaller than the other kids?"

The honest answer: "Kids grow on different timelines. Some finish early, some finish later. Right now your body is working on other things, and it will get to the growing part on its own schedule. That is the truth as far as anyone can tell."

If he presses: "We are also tracking it with the doctor, just so we know how you are doing. If anything ever needs attention, we will see it."

He does not want to try out for a team because "I am too small"

First: Do not argue. "I hear you. That is a real thing to be worried about."

Then: "What matters most to you about playing this year? Is it that you get to play at all, or that you get a certain position, or something else?"

Only if it feels right: "There are also grown men in [sport] who are your kind of build. If it would help, I can pull up a few."

These scripts are starting points, not scripts to read from. You know your son.
