What to monitor

Signed in as

General guidance. The items below apply broadly. They are not a diagnosis. Anything that concerns you is worth mentioning at your son's next well-child visit.

Puberty signals to watch

Puberty timing varies. The typical range for boys is a first sign somewhere between ages 9 and 14 (AAP). Tracking these signals helps you and your pediatrician understand how much growth may still be ahead.

  • Testicular volume. Enlargement past prepubertal size is usually the first sign of puberty in boys (Endocrine Society clinical guidance). Pediatricians assess this at well-child visits.
  • Pubic hair. Usually follows testicular enlargement by several months.
  • Voice change and body composition. Voice deepening, broader shoulders, and muscle growth typically arrive later in puberty.
  • Growth acceleration. The pubertal growth spurt in boys usually happens later than in girls, often between ages 12 and 15.

Sources: American Academy of Pediatrics (healthychildren.org), Endocrine Society Clinical Practice Guidelines.

Growth velocity red flags

Velocity means how much your son is growing per year. It matters more than any single measurement. Discuss with your pediatrician if you notice:

  • Growth of less than about 4 cm (1.6 in) per year in a school-age child before puberty (general guidance).
  • A previously steady percentile dropping across two or more major channels on the CDC growth chart (for example, going from the 25th to below the 5th).
  • Height that appears to plateau for 6 to 12 months in a preteen who is not yet in puberty.
  • A large mismatch between your son's height percentile and the mid-parental height estimate (average of parents' heights, plus or minus adjustment).

Sources: CDC Growth Charts (cdc.gov/growthcharts), Endocrine Society guidance on short stature.

When to ask about a specialist referral

A pediatric endocrinologist is the specialist for growth concerns. Reasons to ask about a referral include:

  • Height consistently below the 3rd percentile for age (general guidance).
  • A significant drop in growth velocity as described above.
  • Height that is more than about 2 standard deviations below the mid-parental target.
  • Delayed puberty: no signs by age 14 in boys (Endocrine Society).
  • Symptoms alongside short stature: chronic fatigue, headaches, vision changes, unexplained weight loss or gain.

How to ask, without triggering a shutdown. Bring your Growth Record and specific numbers. Ask, "Given his velocity and mid-parental target, do you think an endocrinology consult would be useful?" That framing is much more likely to open a real conversation than asking for a specific treatment by name.

What is not a red flag on its own

  • Being smaller than classmates. Class size varies a lot at every age.
  • A single low percentile measurement. Trend matters more than one point.
  • Late puberty on its own if velocity is normal and family history includes late bloomers.
