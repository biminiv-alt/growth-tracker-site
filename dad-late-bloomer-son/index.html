Dad Was a Late Bloomer. Will My Son Be One Too?

Constitutional delay runs in families. If dad went through puberty later than his peers, the odds his son follows the same pattern are meaningfully higher.

The short answer

Yes, likely. Constitutional delay of growth and puberty (CDGP) is one of the most strongly familial patterns in pediatric endocrinology. If a father had CDGP, roughly 50% of his sons show a similar pattern.

Practically: a currently-shorter boy with a late-blooming dad usually does not need extensive workup. Family history alone is one of the strongest pieces of reassurance a pediatrician can offer.

What "dad was a late bloomer" actually means

  • Voice changed noticeably later than peers (often 15-16 vs. 12-14).
  • Major growth spurt in high school rather than middle school.
  • Gained several inches junior and senior year.
  • Ended up at or near his family's expected adult height, on a delayed timeline.

If dad was always shorter and stayed that height as an adult, that is familial short stature, not late blooming. Different pattern.

How strong is the family link?

  • ~50% concordance between fathers with CDGP and their sons.
  • Higher with late-blooming uncles or grandfathers on either side.
  • ~2% base rate of CDGP in the general boy population.

What CDGP looks like in a boy

  • Tracks a low percentile line consistently.
  • Growth velocity normal for age.
  • Late signs of puberty (14+).
  • Delayed bone age.
  • Reaches normal adult height 1-2 years later than typical.

How to gather family history

  1. Ask dad: when did his voice change? When did he start shaving? When was his biggest growth spurt?
  2. Ask about his brothers and father.
  3. Ask on mom's side too.
  4. Rough estimates are fine.

When to still ask for a closer look

  • Percentile crossing lines downward.
  • Growth velocity below age range.
  • Over 14 with zero signs of puberty AND slow growth.
  • He is distressed by the delay.

The treatment question

Some families with confirmed CDGP choose short-course low-dose testosterone (3-6 months) to kick-start the spurt earlier. This does not change final adult height, only timing. Whether that tradeoff makes sense is a family decision with the endocrinologist.
