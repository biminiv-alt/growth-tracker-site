My Son's Height Percentile Has Stayed the Same for Years. Is That Good?

Yes, it is the pattern pediatricians actually want to see. A boy staying near his percentile line year over year is a boy growing normally, whatever that percentile is.

Why "stable percentile" is a good pattern

Percentile is a comparison to the average population at his age. Staying at the same percentile means he is growing at the same relative rate as his peers. Whether that percentile is the 10th or the 90th, tracking a line is a healthy growth signal.

What pediatricians actually watch for is crossing lines, especially downward. A boy dropping from the 50th to the 25th to the 10th over a couple of years is a different pattern than a boy who has been at the 10th all along.

What "stable" means practically

  • Fluctuating within a 5-10 point band year to year is stable. Percentile calculations are imprecise, especially at the low and high ends.
  • Consistent tracking near a specific line (say, always between the 15th and 25th) is textbook stable.
  • Big single-year jumps (up or down 20+ points) usually reflect measurement variability rather than actual growth changes. Confirm with a second measurement 3-6 months later.

What might change a stable pattern

During puberty specifically, percentile can shift because of timing:

  • An early developer often moves DOWN in percentile in mid-teens as classmates catch up. This is normal.
  • A late bloomer often moves UP in percentile in mid-teens as he starts growing while early developers finish. Also normal.
  • Both patterns settle into a stable adult-height percentile by 17-18.

When "stable but low" is worth investigating

  • He is below the 3rd percentile AND family height does not explain it (both parents average or taller).
  • His current percentile is well below his mid-parental family target.
  • His growth velocity is below age-appropriate ranges even though percentile appears "stable" (velocity and percentile don't always move together).

None of these cancels the reassurance of a stable line. They are additional signals worth mentioning at his next well visit.

What NOT to worry about

  • A stable low percentile with family history to explain it. This is genetics playing out normally.
  • Small year-to-year wobbles inside a 10-point band. Noise.
  • Being below classmates. Puberty timing spreads boys 3+ years apart.

What to keep doing

  1. Measure every 6 months. Same conditions.
  2. Write it down. Any log works.
  3. Calculate velocity once a year. Compare to age ranges.
  4. Bring the log to well visits. Especially useful during the puberty years.
