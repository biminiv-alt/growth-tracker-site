How the mid-parental height formula works

The mid-parental height formula is the most widely used clinical estimate for a child's adult height. It uses only two inputs: both biological parents' heights.

For boys: (Father's height + Mother's height + 13 cm) ÷ 2
(Or, in inches: (Father's + Mother's + 5 in) ÷ 2)

The formula adjusts for the average height difference between adult men and women. The typical confidence range is ±8.5 cm (±3.3 in), meaning about two thirds of children fall within that window.

Why family height is the strongest single signal

Genetics accounts for about 80% of the variation in adult height. Parents' heights are the closest proxy for that genetic contribution â closer than any other single input. Studies of twins and family trees consistently confirm this.

That said, the mid-parental estimate is a range, not a guarantee. About one in three children ends up outside the ±8.5 cm window, and the range doesn't account for nutrition, illness, growth-timing differences, or genetic variation between siblings.

What this calculator doesn't tell you

It doesn't tell you where your son is right now on the growth curve. A boy tracking well below his mid-parental target at age 11 may catch up later, or may need a closer look. That trend â measured over time â is more diagnostic than any single prediction. The height percentile calculator shows you where he sits today; the Growth Check puts both together.

Frequently asked questions

How accurate is the mid-parental height formula?

The ±8.5 cm range captures roughly 68% of children (one standard deviation). About 95% of children fall within ±17 cm (±6.7 in). So it's a useful ballpark, not a prediction. Pediatricians use it as one data point among several â not on its own.

What if the parents are unusually tall or short?

The formula still works, but the confidence range widens. Genetic variation is greater at the extremes, and children of very tall or very short parents are more likely to end up closer to the population mean (a phenomenon called regression to the mean).

Does the mother's or father's height matter more for a boy?

Roughly equally. The formula weights both parents' heights the same and adds a fixed offset (13 cm for boys) to account for the average adult male-female height difference.

Should we use step-parent or adoptive parent heights?

No. Use biological parents' heights whenever possible. If one biological parent's height is unknown, the formula will be less reliable â a rough workaround is to use the population average (about 5'9" for U.S. men, 5'4" for U.S. women), but be aware the confidence range widens substantially.

My son is already outside the predicted range. What does that mean?

It could mean nothing â the formula misses one in three kids by definition. It could also mean he's still growing into or out of his family's range as puberty timing plays out. Sustained tracking well below (or well above) family expectation over years is more meaningful than a snapshot. If you're concerned, that's a conversation to have with his pediatrician.
