My Son Is 15 and Still Has Not Had a Growth Spurt. What Should We Do?

By The Growth Path Editorial Team · Reviewed September 2026

At 15 with no growth spurt yet, the answer is not always "just wait." Here is what the pediatrician visit should cover, what bone age reveals, and when hormone therapy is worth discussing.

15 without a growth spurt IS worth a visit

Unlike 14, where "let's watch it" is often reasonable, 15 without any visible growth spurt is the age where most pediatricians will actively investigate. This is not because something is likely wrong. It is because the treatment window is narrowing. Constitutional delay is still the most common explanation. But at 15, you want to know for sure rather than assume.

What the pediatrician should cover

A thorough visit at 15 for no-spurt should include:

  • Physical exam for any signs of puberty (Tanner staging). This tells you whether puberty has quietly started.
  • Growth chart review for the last 4-5 years to see whether he is tracking a low line consistently or has been dropping.
  • Bone age x-ray (single wrist image). This is the highest-value single test at this age.
  • Basic blood work to rule out thyroid, growth hormone, or other systemic causes. Common panels include TSH, IGF-1, LH, FSH, and testosterone.

If any of these show a red flag, referral to pediatric endocrinology is the next step. If everything looks like constitutional delay, the choice becomes wait vs. treat.

What "treatment" for constitutional delay actually is

For a boy with confirmed constitutional delay who is bothered by being small for his age, pediatric endocrinologists sometimes offer low-dose testosterone therapy as a short course (usually 3-6 months). What it does:

  • Kick-starts secondary sexual development.
  • Triggers the growth spurt earlier than it would otherwise happen.
  • Does NOT typically change final adult height, which is genetic. It changes WHEN he reaches it.

This is a real medical decision, not cosmetic. Whether it makes sense depends on how much the delay is affecting him socially and emotionally, family preferences, and the endocrinologist's read. It is not always recommended. But it is a legitimate option for the right kid, and it exists because pediatric endocrinology has decades of experience with it.

Growth hormone therapy is a separate, more involved conversation. It is generally NOT indicated for constitutional delay alone. It is used when there is a documented growth hormone deficiency, which is rare.

The "just wait" option is legitimate too

Many families choose to wait, especially if:

  • There is clear family history of late puberty on either side.
  • Bone age is significantly delayed, meaning plenty of growth is still coming.
  • The boy himself is not distressed by the delay.
  • Blood work and physical exam look normal.

The tradeoff of waiting is another 1-2 years of being smaller than peers. The tradeoff of treating is a medical intervention. Neither is wrong.

What to bring to the pediatrician visit

  1. His growth chart or measurements from the last 4-5 years.
  2. Family history: when Dad, brothers, uncles, grandfathers went through puberty.
  3. Notes on any signs of puberty you have noticed (subtle voice change, acne, body odor, height acceleration in the last 6 months).
  4. His feelings on it. If he is fine with it, that changes the calculation. If it is affecting him, that also changes the calculation.

What NOT to do

  • Do not buy over-the-counter "growth boosters" or supplements. Nothing sold without a prescription has been shown to help.
  • Do not delay past 15 waiting for it to sort itself out. The window for hormone-based interventions to affect timing narrows with age.
  • Do not assume "he'll be fine." He usually will, but the visit is how you know.
