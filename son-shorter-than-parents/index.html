My Son Is Shorter Than Both Parents. Will He Catch Up?

By The Growth Path Editorial Team · Reviewed September 2026

Most likely yes, if he is not yet full-grown. Here is why comparing a still-growing boy to two adult parents almost always makes him look shorter than he is on track to be.

The comparison is misleading if he is under 18

If your son is still growing, comparing him to your and your partner's adult heights is comparing him to his own future, not his present. A 14-year-old boy who is 5' 4" and shorter than a 5' 10" father is not "shorter than dad." He is on his way to something. The right comparison is his current height against the CDC percentile chart for his exact age, plus his family-height target range.

What the mid-parental height formula predicts

The standard formula pediatric endocrinologists use for boys:

  • (Mother's height in cm + Father's height in cm + 13) / 2 = midpoint of expected adult range
  • Plus and minus 8.5 cm gives the confidence band (which covers roughly the middle 66% of actual outcomes)

For example, mom at 5' 4" (163 cm) and dad at 5' 10" (178 cm) predicts:

  • Midpoint: (163 + 178 + 13) / 2 = 177 cm, or about 5' 9.7"
  • Range: 168.5 to 185.5 cm, or roughly 5' 6.4" to 6' 1"

That range is wide on purpose. Real outcomes span an even wider window because of puberty timing, nutrition, and genetic recombination. But the midpoint is a defensible starting expectation.

Why boys often end up taller than the "shorter parent"

The +13 cm term in the formula is the average difference between adult male and female heights. That is what accounts for boys usually being taller than their mothers, and often taller than dad if dad is around average height and mom is above average.

A boy whose mother is 5' 6" and father is 5' 8" has a mid-parental target around 5' 11". He can very reasonably end up 2 inches taller than dad and 5 inches taller than mom, and that is completely on model.

When "shorter than parents" IS worth investigating

The question changes if:

  • His current percentile is materially below what family height predicts. A boy in the 10th percentile with parents whose family target is the 50th is a genuine 40-point gap and worth understanding.
  • He has been crossing percentile lines downward, not just tracking on a low line.
  • He is past 17 and clearly closer to done, and still well below both parents.
  • He has slow growth velocity in addition to the height gap.

Any of these makes it worth checking with the pediatrician, primarily to look at bone age and rule out growth hormone or thyroid issues. These conditions are treatable when caught, which is why the check exists.

Grandparents matter more than you think

Height is not just about mom and dad. It regresses toward the mean of the extended family. If both sets of grandparents were on the shorter side, a shorter boy is more expected. If grandparents were tall on both sides and both parents ended up on the shorter side of their potential, a boy can genuinely end up taller than either parent by 3-4 inches.

This is called regression to the family mean. It is why "he's taller than both of us" is more common than the mid-parental midpoint alone would suggest, especially in tall-grandparent families.

The realistic timeline for catching up

Boys typically reach 95%+ of their adult height by 17-18. Some late bloomers grow into their early 20s, but visible catch-up happens mostly during the puberty growth spurt (usually 12-16). If your son is:

  • Under 13: comparing to adult parents is roughly like comparing a rough draft to a finished essay.
  • 13-15: he's likely in the middle of the change that will define his adult height.
  • 15-17: still meaningful growth ahead, but the arc is more predictable.
  • 17+: most catch-up growth has happened. Compare his current height to the mid-parental range to see whether he is on track.

What to actually do

  1. Calculate his mid-parental range using the formula above (or use our Family Height Calculator).
  2. Check his current percentile against CDC data (or use our Growth Check).
  3. If those two pictures roughly match, he is on track. If percentile is well below the family range, that is the gap to investigate.
