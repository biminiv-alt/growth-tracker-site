How Much Do Boys Usually Grow After Puberty Starts?

By The Growth Path Editorial Team · Reviewed September 2026

From the first puberty signs to fully adult height, most boys gain 10 to 14 inches over roughly 4 to 6 years. The middle two years account for most of it.

The typical number

Total pubertal growth in boys averages 10 to 14 inches (about 25 to 35 cm). The window is roughly 4 to 6 years from earliest signs to skeletal maturity. Peak growth velocity (the spurt itself) usually delivers about half of that total in just 2 years.

  • First 6-12 months of puberty: weight gain, small height uptick. Maybe 2-3 inches.
  • Peak spurt (usually 13-15): 3-4 inches per year for 2 years. Half of total pubertal growth happens here.
  • Late puberty (16-18): 1-2 inches per year, slowing to a stop.

Why the range is so wide

Two boys can each grow a totally normal amount and end up 4 inches apart, simply because one started puberty at 10 and the other at 13. Earlier starters have more time in the "before" phase where they are growing 2 inches a year. Later starters compress everything into a shorter, more dramatic spurt. Total inches are similar; distribution over time is different.

What predicts how much runway he has

  • His height when puberty started. A boy who was 4' 10" at first puberty sign will land taller than one who was 4' 6".
  • Family height. Mid-parental height (mom + dad + 13) / 2 for boys is the strongest single predictor.
  • Where he is on the timeline now. Peak velocity behind him means less runway.

Signs the biggest growth is still ahead

  • He is 11-13 with early puberty signs and not much height change yet.
  • Shoe size climbing.
  • Weight up, height about to catch.

Signs peak growth is happening now

  • 3-4 inches in the last year.
  • Pants too short every few months.
  • Constant hunger and sleep.

When to talk to the pediatrician

If puberty has been progressing for 12+ months and he has grown less than 2 inches in that year, ask his pediatrician. The velocity should be visibly higher than pre-puberty. Underperforming velocity during puberty is a legitimate reason to look closer.
