What Are the Signs of Growth Hormone Deficiency in Boys?

By The Growth Path Editorial Team · Reviewed September 2026

Growth hormone deficiency is uncommon. Slowing growth velocity, delayed bone age, and other clinical findings matter more than short stature alone. Here is what pediatricians actually look for.

How common is GHD, really?

Growth hormone deficiency (GHD) affects roughly 1 in 3,500 to 1 in 10,000 children. Most short boys do not have GHD. Familial short stature and constitutional delay of growth and puberty are far more common explanations for being shorter than average.

Signs pediatricians actually look for

  1. Growth velocity below age-appropriate range. Pre-puberty: under 2 inches per year over 12 months. Active puberty: under 1.5 inches per year. This is the single strongest signal.
  2. Height crossing percentile lines downward over 12+ months. Not just being short, but drifting from higher to lower percentiles.
  3. Delayed bone age on wrist x-ray. Bone age often 2+ years behind chronological age.
  4. Chubbier body composition despite normal calorie intake. Increased body fat relative to muscle is a classic GHD sign.
  5. Delayed puberty on top of short stature. Puberty not started by age 14.
  6. Prominent forehead, smaller face, slower dental development. Not always present, but part of the picture.
  7. Family history is NOT a match. Both parents average or tall, but the boy is markedly short.

Signs that are NOT GHD

  • Just being short with tracking-on-percentile growth.
  • Late puberty in a boy with dad or brothers who also had late puberty.
  • Short parents.
  • Normal growth velocity even if the current percentile is low.

How GHD is diagnosed

A pediatric endocrinologist typically orders:

  • IGF-1 blood test (surrogate marker for growth hormone activity).
  • IGFBP-3 blood test (a companion marker, especially in younger kids).
  • Bone age x-ray of the left wrist.
  • Growth hormone stimulation test (definitive test, requires timed blood draws).
  • MRI of the pituitary if GHD is confirmed, to look for a structural cause.

What treatment looks like

Daily subcutaneous growth hormone injections, typically for years. Started before puberty produces the best final-height results. Effect varies significantly by cause, timing, and adherence. This is a specialist decision.

When to ask for a pediatric endocrine referral

  • Height below the 3rd percentile plus growth velocity below age-appropriate range.
  • Percentile crossing lines downward over 12+ months.
  • Height significantly below what family predicts (mid-parental height minus more than 8.5 cm).
  • Any of the classic GHD signs above.
