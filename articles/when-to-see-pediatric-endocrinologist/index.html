When to see a pediatric endocrinologist about your son's growth

By The Growth Path · September 21, 2026 · 6 min read

Your pediatrician has said "he'll probably catch up" enough times that you've stopped believing it. You're wondering if you should push for a specialist. Here is when the answer is yes, and what actually happens if you go.

The five referral signals

Any one of these is a valid reason to ask your pediatrician for a referral to a pediatric endocrinologist. Bring this list to the appointment. You don't need permission to advocate for your kid.

1. He's below the 3rd percentile for height

That's the clinical threshold for short stature. Fewer than 3 out of every 100 boys his age are shorter. Even if he's tracking his own curve, sub-3rd deserves an evaluation.

2. His growth velocity has slowed

Under 2 inches per year past age 4 (before puberty). Or a drop of more than 2 percentile bands over 12 months (say, from the 50th to under the 20th). Slowed velocity is a stronger signal than a single low percentile.

3. His current percentile is far below family-height prediction

Run the mid-parental formula. If your family math says 5'10" and he's currently on the 10th percentile at age 10, there's a gap worth investigating.

4. He's 14 or older with no signs of puberty

By age 14, most boys show at least one physical sign of puberty (testicular enlargement, pubic hair, voice deepening). Age 14 with nothing is the clinical definition of delayed puberty. Most cases turn out to be constitutional delay, but a specialist should confirm.

5. Anything else in the workup is off

Chronic fatigue, unexplained weight loss or gain, hypoglycemia symptoms, headaches with vision changes, or lab abnormalities on thyroid or IGF-1. These are pediatric endocrinology territory, not general pediatrics.

What the pediatric endocrinologist actually does

The first visit typically covers:

  • Full growth history review. Bring past measurements if you have them.
  • Family history (both sides, back to grandparents when possible). Dad's puberty timing is especially important.
  • Physical exam including Tanner staging.
  • Review of any prior labs. If none, they'll order a workup.
  • A plan. Usually one of three: (a) monitor for 6 months and re-measure, (b) order specific tests, (c) start treatment if something has already been confirmed.

The tests to expect

A first-pass endocrine workup for short stature usually includes:

  • Complete blood count and metabolic panel. Screens for chronic disease.
  • Thyroid function (TSH and Free T4). Hypothyroidism can slow growth.
  • IGF-1 and IGFBP-3. The main lab screen for growth hormone deficiency.
  • Celiac panel. Celiac disease can cause growth failure even without gut symptoms.
  • Bone age X-ray of the left hand. Shows skeletal maturity vs. calendar age.
  • Karyotype (chromosome analysis). Usually only if physical features suggest a syndrome.

If those come back suggestive of growth hormone deficiency, the specialist orders a growth hormone stimulation test. That's a several-hour test done in office.

What insurance usually covers

Most US insurance plans cover a pediatric endocrinology consult if your pediatrician makes the referral. The workup labs are usually covered. Growth hormone treatment (if it comes to that) is covered for confirmed deficiency but often requires prior authorization.

Growth hormone treatment for idiopathic short stature (no confirmed cause) is more variable. Ask about coverage before treatment starts.

How to find a good one

  • Ask your pediatrician for their preferred referral. They usually know the local specialists.
  • Check your insurance's in-network list.
  • If you're in a metro area, most major academic medical centers have pediatric endocrinology.
  • Verify the doctor is board-certified in pediatric endocrinology (not just adult endocrinology).

What to bring to the first visit

  • All prior growth measurements you can find (well-child visit records).
  • Both biological parents' adult heights.
  • Dad's puberty timing if he remembers.
  • Any prior lab results.
  • Your list of specific questions. Do not walk in without them.

What the visit will not do

It won't give you a definitive answer in one appointment. Growth is a longitudinal question. Expect a plan, not a diagnosis, on day one.
