Bone Age X-Ray: What It Measures, What It Tells You, and When to Ask

By The Growth Path Editorial Team · Reviewed September 2026

If your pediatrician mentioned a bone age X-ray, or your endocrinologist has already ordered one, you are probably wondering what it actually measures and what the number really tells you. Bone age is one of the most useful tools in pediatric growth medicine, and also one of the most misunderstood.

Here is what it is, what it can answer, and what it cannot.

What a bone age X-ray actually is

It is a single X-ray of the left hand and wrist. A radiologist or endocrinologist compares it to a reference atlas (usually the Greulich and Pyle atlas or the Tanner-Whitehouse method) and reads off an approximate skeletal age. The whole thing takes about five minutes and involves less radiation than a cross-country flight.

The point is not to look at how big his hand bones are. It is to look at the pattern and density of the growth plates and small bones, which mature in a predictable order.

What the number means

His bone age is not his real age. It is a measure of how physically mature his skeleton is compared to a reference set of kids his sex. There are three possible relationships:

  • Bone age matches chronological age. He is developing on schedule. Nothing surprising.
  • Bone age is younger than chronological age. He has more growing time left than his birth certificate suggests. This is the pattern in constitutional delay of growth and puberty, and in some hormonal conditions.
  • Bone age is older than chronological age. He is maturing early and has less growing time left. This is the pattern in precocious puberty and in some kids who were tall for their age when younger.

A rough rule of thumb: a boy with a bone age meaningfully younger than his chronological age has more room and time to keep growing. A boy whose bone age has already caught up or passed his chronological age is closer to his final height than the number of birthdays suggests.

What questions a bone age can help answer

Endocrinologists order a bone age when they need to answer one of these:

  • Is he short because he is a late bloomer, or because he has less growth left? Bone age is the single best tool for this.
  • Roughly how much taller might he get? Bone age plugs into prediction methods like Bayley-Pinneau, Roche-Wainer-Thissen, and Khamis-Roche to estimate adult height.
  • Is puberty coming early or late? Bone age tracks puberty timing reasonably well.
  • Is a suspected condition (CDGP, GHD, precocious puberty, familial short stature) consistent with what we are seeing?

What a bone age cannot tell you

This is where a lot of families are misled. A bone age X-ray:

  • Cannot tell you his exact adult height. It gives a prediction with a confidence range of a couple of inches in either direction.
  • Cannot diagnose growth hormone deficiency by itself. That takes bloodwork and often stimulation testing.
  • Cannot predict how quickly he will finish growing. It gives an estimate, not a schedule.
  • Cannot rule out every underlying condition. It is one piece of a workup, not the whole thing.

If someone (a doctor, an online calculator, a supplement site) tells you a bone age X-ray gives an exact adult height, treat that as a red flag.

How predictions actually work

Adult height predictions based on bone age are statistical, not literal. The most common methods use current height, current bone age, and sometimes weight and parental heights, then compare against a large reference dataset. Typical accuracy is give or take about 2 inches at the 95 percent confidence level.

For most families, that is still useful. Knowing your 12-year-old is likely to end up between 5'8" and 6'0" is a lot more actionable than not knowing.

When to ask for one

Bone age is not routinely ordered. Most kids never need one. Reasons a pediatrician or endocrinologist might order one include:

  • Height below the 3rd percentile
  • Growth velocity under 5 cm per year after age 4
  • Signs of early puberty (before age 9 in boys)
  • No signs of puberty by age 14
  • Big mismatch between his height and his parents' heights
  • Family concern that warrants sorting out CDGP from other causes

If your pediatrician is dismissive but your gut says something is off, asking for a bone age X-ray is a specific, reasonable request. If it comes back matching his chronological age, that is real information. If it comes back significantly younger or older, that is also real information. Either way, you leave with more than you had walking in.

Standard endocrine referral thresholds are height below the 3rd percentile, growth velocity under 5 cm per year past age 4, or no signs of puberty by age 14. A bone age X-ray is often ordered at the first pediatric endocrinology visit.

What to expect at the appointment

Practical stuff:

  • He places his left hand palm-down on a plate for a few seconds.
  • No prep, no fasting, no sedation.
  • Radiation exposure is very low, comparable to a few days of normal background radiation.
  • Results usually come back within a few days.

The follow-up visit is where the real conversation happens. Bring your questions written down. Ask what the number means for his predicted adult height, what the confidence range is, and what (if anything) changes based on the result.
