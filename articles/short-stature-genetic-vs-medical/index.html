Short Stature in Boys: Genetic, Constitutional, or Medical?

By The Growth Path Editorial Team · Reviewed September 2026

If your son is shorter than his classmates and you have been told "he will catch up," or "it runs in the family," or "let's watch it," you are probably left wondering which of those is actually true for him. Pediatricians think about short stature in three broad buckets. Knowing which one applies to your son changes what happens next, and whether anything needs to happen at all.

The Three Buckets

Most boys who are short for their age fall into one of three categories:

  1. Familial short stature. Short parents, short son, on his own healthy growth curve.
  2. Constitutional delay of growth and puberty. A late bloomer whose whole clock is running behind, but who will land at a normal adult height.
  3. Pathological short stature. An underlying medical cause. Less common than the other two, but the reason a workup exists.

The vast majority of short boys fall into the first two categories. Neither of those requires treatment. The third is worth ruling out because some of the causes are treatable, and treatment works better when it starts before the growth plates fuse.

Familial Short Stature

This is the simplest one. A short boy with short parents, whose growth curve tracks steadily along its own percentile, and who goes through puberty on a normal schedule, is just going to be short. His body is doing exactly what his genes told it to do.

The signs of familial short stature:

  • One or both biological parents are short.
  • His height percentile has been consistent since infancy (say, 5th percentile since age 2 and still 5th today).
  • His predicted adult height, using mid-parental height, matches where he is tracking.
  • Puberty is on schedule.
  • Growth velocity is normal for his age.

Nothing is wrong. He is simply on a shorter track. No workup is needed. No treatment will change the outcome. This category is common, and it is not a diagnosis so much as a description.

Constitutional Delay of Growth and Puberty

This is the classic "late bloomer." A boy with constitutional delay is short throughout childhood, starts puberty late, hits his growth spurt late, and often ends up at a normal adult height, just years later than his classmates.

The pattern:

  • Shorter than peers from early childhood.
  • Normal growth velocity, but tracking below the average curve.
  • Puberty starts late (often 13 to 15, sometimes later).
  • Bone age on an X-ray is younger than his chronological age.
  • Strong family history: a dad, uncle, or grandfather who was "the short kid" until college, then grew.

The bone age X-ray is the key. If your son is 13 and his hand X-ray reads like an 11-year-old's, that is the signature of constitutional delay. It means his growth plates still have plenty of runway, and once puberty arrives, he will spurt like everyone else, just later.

Some families choose to observe. Others opt for a short course of testosterone to nudge puberty along, mostly for social and emotional reasons. It does not change the final adult height. If you want to know how tall he is likely to be, the prediction formulas already account for a late bone age.

Pathological Short Stature

This is the category everyone wants to rule out. It is less common than the first two, but it is the reason short stature deserves a real look. Medical causes of short stature include:

  • Growth hormone deficiency. The pituitary makes too little growth hormone. Treatable with growth hormone injections, and treatment works best when started early.
  • Hypothyroidism. Undertreated or untreated low thyroid function slows growth dramatically.
  • Celiac disease. Undiagnosed celiac can present as failure to grow, sometimes with no gut symptoms at all.
  • Chronic illnesses. Kidney disease, inflammatory bowel disease, and other chronic conditions can suppress growth.
  • Genetic syndromes. Turner, Noonan, and related conditions. Rare, but worth screening for in the right context.
  • Undernutrition. Not just calories, but specific deficits like iron, zinc, or vitamin D over a long period.
  • Psychosocial factors. Severe emotional stress can suppress growth hormone. Rare, but real.

The reason a pediatric endocrinologist gets involved is to run through this list systematically. Bloodwork, a bone age X-ray, and a careful history usually sort it out. Most workups come back reassuring, which is worth a lot on its own.

How Pediatricians Tell Them Apart

The three-way sort usually comes down to a handful of data points:

  • Growth curve shape. A steady low curve suggests familial short stature. A curve that drops across percentiles is a red flag for a medical cause. A curve that stays low but keeps a normal slope, then spurts late, is constitutional delay.
  • Growth velocity. Normal is at least 5 cm per year between ages 4 and puberty. Consistently below that is worth investigating.
  • Bone age vs chronological age. A bone age that matches suggests familial short stature. A younger bone age suggests constitutional delay. A significantly younger bone age combined with poor velocity may indicate a medical cause.
  • Family history. Short parents lean familial. Late-bloomer relatives lean constitutional.
  • Puberty timing. On time with everyone else suggests familial. Very late suggests constitutional. Absent by 14 is a workup trigger.

When to talk to a pediatric endocrinologist: Ask for a referral if your son's height is below the 3rd percentile, if his growth velocity has been below 5 cm per year past age 4, if he crosses down two or more percentile lines on his growth chart, or if he has no signs of puberty by age 14. These are the same triggers pediatric endocrinologists use, and catching a treatable cause before the growth plates fuse is the whole point of an early evaluation.

What the Workup Looks Like

If your pediatrician refers you, expect something like this:

  1. A careful history and physical, including puberty staging.
  2. A hand X-ray for bone age.
  3. Bloodwork: thyroid, celiac panel, complete blood count, kidney and liver function, IGF-1 (a proxy for growth hormone activity), and sometimes vitamin D and iron studies.
  4. Depending on those results, sometimes a growth hormone stimulation test or genetic testing.

Most workups end with, "everything looks normal, this is constitutional delay or familial short stature, let's watch it." That is a good outcome. It means you can stop wondering.

What This Means for You

If your son is short and everything else looks normal, statistics say he is most likely a familial or constitutional case. He may end up shorter than average and healthy, or he may be tracking to a normal adult height on a delayed schedule. Either way, no treatment is likely to change much.

If something in the picture is off (a dropping percentile, low velocity, absent puberty by 14, or a symptom you cannot explain) then the workup is worth doing. Not because something is definitely wrong, but because you will know either way, and you will have time to act if it matters.
