Constitutional Delay of Growth and Puberty (CDGP): A Plain-English Guide

If a pediatrician or endocrinologist has used the term "constitutional delay of growth and puberty," or CDGP, and you left the appointment more confused than when you walked in, you are not alone. CDGP is the medical name for what most families call being a late bloomer. It is one of the most common reasons a boy is shorter than his peers in middle school, and it is also one of the more reassuring diagnoses a family can get.

Here is what it actually means, how it is diagnosed, and what the research says about where these boys tend to land.

The plain-English version

CDGP means his growth clock is set a little later than average. His bones may be chronologically 12 but developmentally 10. He will hit puberty later, grow later, and stop growing later than his peers, and his final adult height will usually land in the range you would expect from his parents.

It is a variation of normal, not a disease.

How common it is

CDGP is the single most common cause of short stature and delayed puberty in boys. Estimates vary, but roughly 2 percent of kids fit the strict definition, and boys are diagnosed with it far more often than girls. It also tends to run in families. If Dad was a late bloomer, or Mom got her period late, there is a good chance you are looking at CDGP.

How it gets diagnosed

There is no single blood test for CDGP. It is a diagnosis of pattern, not a lab value. A pediatric endocrinologist typically looks at:

  • Growth chart pattern. A boy with CDGP is short, but he is growing at a normal rate along a lower percentile line. His curve looks like a parallel line, not a flattening one.
  • Family history. Late puberty in a parent or sibling is a strong clue.
  • Bone age X-ray. A boy with CDGP has a bone age that is meaningfully younger than his chronological age, often by 2 to 4 years. This is the single most useful test.
  • Physical exam. Signs of early puberty may be delayed but should be starting by the mid-teens at the latest.
  • Labs. Thyroid function, IGF-1, and sometimes a full puberty panel to rule out other causes.

The diagnosis is essentially "he is short and late, but everything else looks normal, and his bone age matches his stage."

What the research says about final height

This is the part parents most want to know. Studies following boys with CDGP into adulthood consistently find:

  • Most reach a final adult height within their mid-parental target range.
  • The average final adult height in these cohorts is often slightly below the mid-parental target (typically 1 to 2 inches shorter), but individual results vary widely.
  • Boys with CDGP have a longer growth window than average and often keep growing into their late teens.

Translation: most CDGP boys end up right about where their genes suggest they will, just later than everyone else.

What "watch and wait" actually looks like

For most boys with CDGP, the recommendation is monitoring rather than treatment. That usually means:

  • Height and weight measurements every 6 months
  • Repeat bone age X-ray every 1 to 2 years
  • Tracking pubertal stage at each visit
  • Checking labs if the pattern changes

If puberty has not started by age 14, some endocrinologists offer a short course of low-dose testosterone to kickstart puberty. This does not change final adult height, but it can help socially and emotionally when a boy is falling significantly behind his peers.

When it is not CDGP

The tricky part is that CDGP looks similar to a few other things worth ruling out:

  • Familial short stature (short parents, short kid, normal puberty timing) is often confused with CDGP. The bone age is what tells them apart. In familial short stature, bone age matches chronological age.
  • Growth hormone deficiency shows a slowing growth velocity, not just a lower percentile.
  • Hypothyroidism, celiac disease, and chronic illness can all mimic CDGP.
  • Kallmann syndrome and other forms of hypogonadotropic hypogonadism cause puberty not to start at all, rather than start late.

This is why the workup matters, even when the answer turns out to be "he is a late bloomer."

A pediatric endocrinology referral is reasonable if his height falls below the 3rd percentile, his growth velocity is under 5 cm per year after age 4, or he shows no signs of puberty by age 14. Bone age is often the most useful single test for sorting CDGP from other causes.

The hard part of waiting

Even when CDGP is the answer, watching your son be the shortest kid in his grade for years is genuinely hard. He notices. His friends notice. You notice at every school event. Getting a name for what is happening, and a reasonable expectation of where he will land, often helps more than vague reassurance.

Most short-for-their-age boys are late bloomers and catch up. That does not make the middle years easy, but it does make them survivable.
