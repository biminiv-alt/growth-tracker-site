Can Undiagnosed Celiac Disease Affect a Boy's Growth?

By The Growth Path Editorial Team · Reviewed September 2026

Yes. Poor growth is one of the earliest and most common signs of undiagnosed celiac disease in children. Here is what to watch for and when to ask about testing.

The short answer

Yes. Celiac disease damages the small intestine, which impairs absorption of nutrients, including protein, calcium, iron, and calories needed for growth. In children, growth failure or delayed puberty is often the first visible clue, sometimes before any obvious digestive symptoms.

  • Roughly 1 in 100 people in the U.S. have celiac disease.
  • Many are undiagnosed. Estimates suggest a large majority of celiac cases go unrecognized for years.
  • In children, "silent celiac" (no clear GI symptoms) is common.

How celiac affects growth

Gluten (a protein in wheat, barley, and rye) triggers an immune response in celiac patients that damages the villi in the small intestine. Villi are what absorb nutrients. When damaged, the child eats normally but does not absorb what he eats. Result: fewer calories and nutrients reaching the bloodstream, and growth slows.

Signs to watch for

  • Percentile crossing lines downward.
  • Slower-than-expected growth velocity over 6-12 months.
  • Delayed puberty.
  • Recurrent stomach aches, bloating, gas, diarrhea, or constipation.
  • Iron-deficiency anemia that does not respond to supplementation.
  • Chronic fatigue or low energy.
  • Family history of celiac, type 1 diabetes, or autoimmune thyroid disease.

Some children have GI symptoms. Many do not. Growth failure alone can be the whole presentation.

How it is diagnosed

The first step is a blood test (tTG-IgA is the most common). If positive, confirmation is usually by endoscopy with a small intestinal biopsy. Important: he needs to be eating gluten regularly for the tests to be accurate. Do not put him on a gluten-free diet before testing.

What happens after diagnosis

Strict lifelong gluten-free diet. Growth typically catches up within 6-12 months. Many children move back toward their family-height target range once absorption is restored.

When to ask the pediatrician about testing

  • Growth velocity below age-appropriate range for a boy who should still be growing.
  • Percentile dropping over 12+ months.
  • Any of the symptoms above, especially with a family history.
  • Delayed puberty.

The tTG-IgA blood test is inexpensive and widely available. Do not skip it just because he has no GI complaints.
